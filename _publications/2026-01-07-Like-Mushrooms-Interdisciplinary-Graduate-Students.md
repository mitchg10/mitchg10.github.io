plain_language_summary: "Graduate students whose work sits between fields — here, engineering education, science and technology studies, and computer science — are often told they are valuable, and just as often find that no department, committee, or peer group has a ready category for what they do. This essay draws on my own path from an undergraduate electrical engineering program through two years at General Motors and into a doctorate pursued alongside a computer science master's degree to describe what that in-between position actually demands: a constant, mostly invisible effort to explain one's work in whichever vocabulary the room recognizes. I then propose a metaphor for it. Like fungi, which break down what other organisms discard and connect them through underground networks, interdisciplinary graduate students metabolize what separate fields offer and carry it between them; the question for universities is whether they will provide the conditions under which that can happen."
contribution_summary: "I contribute an autoethnographic account of \"interpretive labor\" as the defining and threatening condition of interdisciplinary graduate study — illustrated through the recursive case of a student whose object of study, expertise, is precisely what committee members, peers, and publication venues recognize differently — and extend it into a fungal metaphor that, read through complex adaptive systems theory, yields concrete recommendations for how institutions might curate \"adaptive space\" for interdisciplinary scholars."
research_questions:
  - "What does interdisciplinary graduate study demand of a student whose object of study, expertise, is precisely what each home discipline recognizes differently?"
  - "How do interdisciplinary graduate students perform, and simultaneously learn, the \"interpretive labor\" required to be legible across engineering education research, science and technology studies, and computer science?"
  - "How might institutions, understood as complex adaptive systems, provide the substrate through which interdisciplinary graduate students catalyze institutional change?"
methods: "This is a scholarly essay rather than an empirical study, and it proceeds autoethnographically: I chart a \"personal geography,\" in Downey and Beddoes' sense, from an undergraduate STS course that first unsettled a conventional picture of engineering design, through software work in General Motors' software-defined vehicle group, to a doctorate in engineering education pursued concurrently with a computer science master's degree. That narrative is read against the interdisciplinary graduate education literature on advising, peer networks, and identity, and through Goffman-derived accounts of interpretive labor, with a comparative table setting engineering education research, computer science, and STS side by side on origins, core concerns, paradigms, methods, units of analysis, and orientation to technology. The closing section turns to mycology and to Uhl-Bien and Arena's complex adaptive systems framework to build the metaphor and derive its institutional consequences."
key_findings: "The central tension I trace is that studying expertise as a relational, interactional accomplishment — the STS and interactionist view — while training in fields that treat it as an individually held possession measured against learning outcomes or working code makes every decision of graduate study an instance of the phenomenon under investigation: the choice of advisor positions one within a particular expertise ecosystem, a committee spanning traditions becomes a site for managing competing ontologies of the very thing being studied, and peer feedback in each community reads the work as belonging to another (a \"manifesto\" in EER, overly normative in STS, a \"weird social science malgum\" in CS). Collins and Evans' \"Problem of Extension,\" in this light, stops being an abstract puzzle and becomes the lived inability to locate where one's own expertise ends and something else begins. The barriers I document are concrete — gatekeeping coursework that counts toward nothing, registration systems with no category for a dual enrollment, the difficulty of finding a computer science advisor willing to work with an engineering education student, and three semesters spent locating a single required course that could be taken remotely — yet I hold that their \"potential\" is real, contingent on the interpretive labor required to navigate them and on the awareness that one is learning that labor while performing it."
implications: "Read through complex adaptive systems theory, research universities are better understood as ecosystems at the edge of chaos than as the hierarchical bureaucracies their administrative structures suggest, and interdisciplinary graduate students function as their mycelium. I accordingly propose that institutions curate \"adaptive space\" substrates in five ways: recognizing freedom from linear disciplinary growth trajectories as a strategic asset rather than an anomaly; treating the \"activation triggers\" and conflicting constraints that interdisciplinary work introduces as opportunities rather than problems to be solved; providing sanctioned holes through disciplinary silos, e.g., by reconsidering committee composition requirements for students whose work needs more than one or two external members; protecting interdisciplinary scholars from the illegibility of formal systems through dedicated in-person and online spaces to meet and share navigation strategies; and building pathways for the fruiting bodies of interdisciplinary work — papers, presentations, grants — to be reintegrated into the institution as forces of change. In a moment when many institutions are turning inward to evaluate their strategic assets, a networked adaptive substrate of interdisciplinary scholars may be what the ecosystem needs, though whether that substrate is cultivated or merely tolerated remains an institutional choice."
