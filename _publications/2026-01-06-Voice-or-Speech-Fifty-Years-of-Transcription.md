plain_language_summary: "Turning a recorded conversation into a written transcript looks like clerical work, but every transcript is the product of choices — whether to keep the \"ums,\" how to mark a pause, how to lay speakers out on the page — and those choices quietly shape what a study is able to find. Automatic speech recognition, the technology behind the transcripts that meeting platforms now generate on their own, makes many of those choices on the researcher's behalf: quickly, cheaply, and largely out of sight. We trace half a century of thinking about transcription, review the evidence that these systems hear some voices considerably better than others, and test whether the notation a transcript uses changes how well an AI language model can interpret it. Our aim is to return transcription to view as something researchers decide rather than something that simply arrives."
contribution_summary: "We synthesize fifty years of transcription scholarship with contemporary evidence of ASR bias to argue that AI-based transcription does not resolve Ochs' \"transcription as theory\" but defers and obscures it, and we give that argument empirical purchase by showing that Jefferson notation measurably improves LLM interpretation of a transcript within what we term the \"transcript-recoverable boundary.\""
methods: "We employ a critical review approach, which goes beyond describing an identified literature toward analysis and conceptual innovation, reading roughly fifty years of transcription scholarship across linguistics, folklore and oral history, anthropology, ethnomethodology, sociolinguistics, and education alongside recent evidence on ASR performance and bias. Two case studies extend the review empirically: in the first, we instruct ASR systems to notate publicly available audio using existing transcription notation systems; in the second, we pass a hand-transcribed scene from Primer (2004) to several LLMs in two forms — a raw transcript stripped of speaker names, company identifiers, and paralinguistic features, and the same scene in Jefferson notation accompanied by a system prompt explaining the conventions — putting fifteen deliberately interpretive questions to each model independently at fixed configuration settings. We tested gpt-oss-120b and gemma-4-31b alongside GLM-5.2 with a thinking budget, so that model scale and reasoning capacity vary alongside notation, and categorized every response by manual inspection as \"Yes,\" \"Kinda,\" or \"No\" against pre-established answers."
key_findings: "The Jefferson-notated prompt-transcript pair outperformed its raw equivalent across all the LLMs we tested, which suggests that notation does not merely alter model responses but improves their discernment when interpreting a transcript; we describe this as a \"strengthening\" effect, and it holds only within what we call the transcript-recoverable boundary, since questions demanding context no transcript carries — the speakers' institutional standing, say — draw hallucinated answers regardless of notation, though the reasoning-enabled model proved better at catching itself before that edge. The historical and empirical strands converge here: ASR systems carry documented performance disparities against AAVE speakers, regional dialects, non-native speakers, and older speakers, and they miss nearly all clinically relevant non-lexical sounds, so a transcript arrives already configured before a researcher reads a word of it. Taken together, the \"good enough, pending check\" standard now common in qualitative work does not dissolve the interpretive problem Ochs named so much as relocate it, from the visible labor of transcribers to the invisible design choices of the engineers and corporations who build, train, and sustain these systems."
implications: "Because a researcher cannot code a transcript they do not have, and cannot have one without something or someone transcribing, transcription remains a vital touchpoint between qualitative researchers and the people, objects, and contexts of their investigation even where it appears minimized and distanced; treating it as a solved problem, or relegating it to a \"fetish,\" would be a grave mistake for the field. Upholding research quality accordingly requires that researchers describe their transcription processes in publications — how recordings were obtained, which ASR systems were used and on what justification, which notation system and with what modifications, how transcripts were verified and corrected, and what the act of transcribing surfaced. Where LLMs enter the workflow, that disclosure extends to how notation was matched to the model, since the context a transcript carries demonstrably shapes what these systems can interpret."
