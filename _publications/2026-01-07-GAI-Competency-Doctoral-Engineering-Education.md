plain_language_summary: "Since ChatGPT arrived, researchers have produced a steady stream of proposals for what students should know about generative AI — \"AI literacy,\" \"AI competency,\" \"AI fluency,\" and so on — but proposals aimed at doctoral engineering students sit awkwardly with how those students actually learn, which happens less in courses than in labs, advising relationships, and the work of producing new knowledge. We read the 31 engineering education conference papers from 2025 that conceptualize AI competency for graduate engineering students, treating each paper's underlying assumptions about what \"competency\" is as the thing worth examining rather than adding yet another framework to the pile. Sorting those assumptions revealed five distinct ways of thinking about AI competency, one of which — treating it as a teachable, add-on skill set — runs through nearly every paper, while the question of who can even access these systems is largely left unspoken. Our aim is to map these positions as a compass for program designers and researchers rather than to prescribe a single answer."
contribution_summary: "We contribute a phenomenographic outcome space — five axiomatic clusters and a three-axis representation derived from their co-occurrence — of how the 2025 engineering education conference literature conceptualizes doctoral student \"AI competency,\" and show that the dominant Instrumental/Modular framing, carried even by ostensibly critical work, is misfit for a population that produces knowledge and sets disciplinary norms rather than merely receiving instruction."
research_questions:
  - "How are engineering education researchers conceptualizing doctoral student \"AI competency\"?"
  - "Which of those conceptualizations fit the distinctive features of the doctoral engineering context?"
methods: "We conducted a critical review with a phenomenographic outcome-space commitment, constructing the corpus through a PRISMA-like screening of roughly 3,000 English-language 2025 conference papers drawn from ASEE PEER, IEEE Xplore, the ACM Digital Library, Engineering Village, and SEFI. Because that yield was intractable for manual screening, we used gpt-oss-120b at low temperature with a prompt iterated against a manually screened validation and test set until it reproduced our inclusion decisions, then manually inspected every model output, arriving at a final corpus of 31 papers. For each paper we reconstructed the argument from problem-space to recommendations, extracted its axiomatic assumptions about social life, epistemology, and the ontology of AI, and synthesized them into roughly seven statements of \"what AI competency is\" — 221 statements in all — which we then clustered inductively by manual comparison, having abandoned an initial vector-embedding approach because it captured surface semantic similarity rather than phenomenographic difference; statement-level assignments were aggregated to paper-level distributions and co-occurrences. The analysis is interpretive and single-coder, which we treat as a stated limit rather than a reason to claim more than the clusters can bear."
key_findings: "Five clusters emerged: Instrumental/Modular (93 statements; 28 of 31 papers, 90.3%), which treats AI competency as a decomposable, teachable skill set added to existing disciplinary knowledge; Critical/Transformative (52; 19 papers, 61.3%), where competency is reflexive, situated, and ethically constitutive; Regulatory/Workforce Alignment (41; 19 papers, 61.3%), legitimated externally by labor markets and policy regimes; Defensive/Preservationist (25; 11 papers, 35.5%), where AI threatens a depletable individual competence; and Resource-Dependent (10; 9 papers, 29.0%), where competency is materially contingent on access. The Instrumental/Modular framing saturates the field, and the most ostensibly critical work concedes it — 16 of the 19 Critical/Transformative papers (84.2%) also carry instrumental statements — so critique is grafted onto an instrumental scaffold rather than replacing it. Only 9 papers engage the unevenness of AI access at all, and the remaining 22 write as if AI were universally available, a position asserted by omission; Regulatory/Workforce and Defensive/Preservationist, as conceptual opposites, co-occur in just 3 papers. From these patterns we derive a three-axis outcome space — embrace versus resist AI, AI use as tool use versus as transformative, and AI as universally available versus resource-dependent — which operates as a compass for locating conceptualizations rather than as a framework prescribing one."
implications: "For program designers, no single cluster should characterize a doctoral program: instrumental modules without critical engagement under-equip students for their norm-setting role, while critical engagement without instrumental skill under-equips them to operate the systems at all, so the justification for which framing applies where matters more than the choice itself, and \"re-learnability\" — dispositions and judgment that transcend any particular AI system — along with AI access conditions as a central object of inquiry should sit at the center of that design. For researchers, the field would benefit less from further frameworks within a single position than from work that holds several positions together, particularly studies of advising and lab-culture dynamics around AI and of the interpretive labor instructors and doctoral students expend bridging divergent AI perspectives across educational settings. For administrators, the prevalence of workforce alignment supports industry partnerships but rests on a future-oriented labor-market argument that flattens alternative perspectives, and the non-modular, non-static nature of AI integration argues against credentialing \"AI competency\" in favor of support structures through which bottom-up norms can take root."
